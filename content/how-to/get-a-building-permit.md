## First question: are you inside city limits?

**A permit is a city matter, and the [City of Trenton](/place/city-of-trenton/) only regulates addresses inside Trenton city limits.** A Trenton, MO 64683 mailing address is not proof that you are inside them. Establish this before anything else, because it decides which office you are dealing with and whether a permit is required at all.

Outside city limits, do not assume either way. Ask the county what applies at your address — the answer is frequently not what people expect, in both directions.

This page prints no fee, processing time or office hour. None has been checked with City Hall.

## What to have ready before you call

- The property address and, if you have it, the parcel identification the [Assessor](/place/grundy-county-assessor/) uses.
- A plain description of the scope: what you are building, altering, demolishing or replacing, and the dimensions.
- Whether the work touches structure, electrical, plumbing, mechanical, or the exterior footprint. These are often separate permits with separate inspections.
- Whether a contractor is doing the work, and their business details.
- A site sketch showing where the work sits relative to property lines. Setback questions come up early.
- Whether the property is in a floodplain. If you do not know, ask.

## The sequence

1. **Call the city and describe the scope.** Ask whether a permit is required at all. Get the answer before you order materials.
2. **Ask which permits.** One project can need more than one, and a sign, a driveway approach or a demolition may each have their own.
3. **Ask what to submit.** Plans, a site sketch, contractor details, an application. Ask what makes an application incomplete, which is the main cause of delay.
4. **Ask about the fee and how it is calculated** — commonly by project value or by square footage. Ask how it is paid.
5. **Submit, and ask when you can start.** Starting is not the same day as submitting.
6. **Book inspections at the stages the city requires,** with the notice they ask for.
7. **Get the final sign-off in writing** and keep it with the property records. It is the document a buyer, an insurer or an appraiser asks for years later.

## Where projects go wrong

**Starting before the permit is issued.** Municipalities commonly charge a higher fee for work already begun, and can require finished work to be opened up for inspection. The most expensive mistake on this page.

**Covering work before it is inspected.** Rough-in inspections happen before insulation, drywall and backfill for a reason. Work covered early gets uncovered, at your cost.

**Assuming the contractor handled it.** Put in writing who pulls the permit and who books the inspections. When both parties assume, nobody does.

**Missing the utility coordination.** A service upgrade, a new connection, a disconnect for demolition or a meter relocation is a separate conversation with [Trenton Municipal Utilities](/place/trenton-municipal-utilities/), with its own lead time. Ask at the same time as the permit, not after the wall is open.

**Ignoring setbacks and zoning.** A permit question and a zoning question are not the same question. Ask both: is this allowed here at all, and what approval does it need?

**Forgetting the assessor.** Work that changes the property shows up in assessment eventually. That is not a reason to skip a permit — an unpermitted addition is a far harder conversation at sale.

## What to ask when you call

1. Does this scope require a permit, and which permits?
2. Who can pull it — the owner, or does it have to be a registered contractor?
3. What exactly do I submit, and what is the usual reason an application comes back?
4. What is the fee, how is it calculated, and when is it paid?
5. Which inspections are required, how much notice do you need, and what happens if I miss one?
6. Are there setback, floodplain or zoning constraints on this parcel I should know about now?

## If it goes wrong

A stop-work order, a failed inspection or a disputed fee goes back to the city department that issued the permit — in person, with the paperwork. What the rules actually say can be checked against the municipal code, published online and linked below. A dispute about property lines is a survey matter before it is a permit matter.
