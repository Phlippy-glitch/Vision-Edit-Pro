## Call 911 first for anything dangerous

**A downed power line, a fire, a smell of gas, or water undermining a road is an emergency call.** Dial 911 and say what you see. Do not route an emergency through this site, any directory, or a business listing — we publish no phone number for police or fire, and we are not a dispatcher.

Everything below is for the non-emergency version: the power is off, the water is out or brown, something is leaking, something is backing up.

## Know your provider before you need it

Inside Trenton city limits, the City of Trenton's own pages describe [Trenton Municipal Utilities](/place/trenton-municipal-utilities/) as the city-owned electric, water and wastewater provider. Outside them, [Grundy Electric Cooperative](/place/grundy-electric-cooperative/) describes itself as a member-owned cooperative serving rural members in northern Missouri and southern Iowa.

Two useful consequences. First, an outage report goes to your provider and nowhere else — telling the wrong one delays nothing except your own restoration. Second, the two run different outage-reporting systems, so the routine you learn at one address does not transfer to the next.

**Do this today, not during the outage:** find your provider's outage and after-hours number, and save it in your phone. A cold search on a dying phone battery in the dark is the worst possible moment to work out who serves you.

## What to have ready

- Your account number and the **service** address, not the mailing address.
- A cross street or a landmark, especially for a rural address.
- When the problem started, and whether it has changed since.
- Whether neighbours are affected. One house out is a different fault from a street out, and saying which you are saves a truck roll.
- For power: whether your main breaker is on, and whether any circuit in the house still has power.
- For water: whether it is no water, low pressure, discoloured water, or water where it should not be.

## Where their equipment ends and yours begins

This is the distinction that decides who pays, and almost every dispute starts here.

**Electric.** Utilities generally maintain the line to a point at the property and the meter itself; equipment past that point is usually the property owner's, which means an electrician rather than a utility crew. If a crew comes out and finds the fault on your side, expect to be told to call an electrician.

**Water.** There is normally a division of responsibility at or near the meter or curb stop. A break in the main is the utility's; a leak in the service line to the house may not be. Ask where that line falls at your address **before** you hire anyone to dig, because a hole in the wrong place is money you cannot get back.

**Sewer.** A backup can be a blockage in your lateral or in the city main, and the responsible party differs. Call before you call a plumber. If the main is the problem, a crew is coming anyway; if it is your lateral, you will at least be paying a plumber for the right job.

We have not confirmed where any of these lines fall in Trenton. Ask, and write down the answer.

## What to ask when you call

1. Is this outage already logged, and do you have an estimated restoration time?
2. Is the fault on your equipment or on mine — and how do I find out without guessing?
3. What is the after-hours number, and is it the same one for water and for electric?
4. If a crew comes out and the problem is on my side, is there a call-out charge?
5. Who handles a streetlight, a water main in the street, or a sewer smell — the utility or the [City](/place/city-of-trenton/)?

Question five is worth asking because street-level infrastructure is often a public works matter rather than a billing-office matter. The city's own department listings name a public works facility, the [Street Barn](/place/trenton-street-barn/), among city locations, and municipal sites usually keep a "how do I…" index that answers this faster than a phone tree.

## After the outage

If food spoiled or equipment failed, ask what the utility's claims process is and what evidence it expects — photographs and timestamps are easier to gather in the first hour than in the second week. If your power came back but something in the house did not, that is an electrician's job, not a second outage call.
